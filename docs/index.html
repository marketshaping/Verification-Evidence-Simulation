Hidden Compute Playground

Imagine a rule says AI labs must report how much computing power (“compute”) they use to train their models, and pay for a permit for each unit. A lab could save money by reporting less than it really used. This page walks through, step by step, when a lab would be tempted to do that and what stops it. Each step has sliders, so you can see the idea working.

Mostly guesses. A few numbers come from real studies; most are placeholders. Use this to understand the logic, not to quote results.

Step 1 of 5

The basic trade: is cheating worth it?

Picture one lab deciding whether to hide one unit of compute. If it hides it, it saves the permit price: 10. But it might get caught. What it expects to pay depends on three things multiplied together: how often it gets checked, how likely a check is to catch it, and how big the fine is.

Step 2 of 5

Two kinds of check, and why small cheating can slip through

Some checks are pass or fail. A satellite photo either shows a hidden data centre or it doesn’t. If you hide anything at all, you risk being caught, so a lab either hides nothing or as much as it can.

Other checks measure. A meter on the chips counts how much compute was used, and the regulator compares that with what the lab reported. But meters are never exactly right. If the regulator raised the alarm at every tiny gap, it would keep accusing honest labs. So it only raises the alarm when the gap is bigger than normal meter error. That leaves room for a lab to under-report a little and stay inside the “normal error” zone.

Typical meter error, as a share of true compute. Our stand-in from nuclear material accounting is about 1%.
The regulator picks this. Fewer false alarms means the alarm line moves further out.

The upshot. Most checks run yearly or all the time, so (with a fine of 5× the saving) the bar is about 20%, not 80%. Step 5 of 5

What this model leaves out

The model is a useful way to think, but it simplifies a lot. Keep these in mind before drawing conclusions.

  • The fine is a guess.

    Everything in step 1 depends on how big the fine is compared with what a lab gains. Under real US laws there’s no permit to save on, and labs also face costs beyond fines (reputation, legal trouble). We don’t know the real ratio yet.

  • Being caught doesn’t always mean being punished.

    The model assumes every caught lab pays. In practice there are appeals and arguments. Iran being caught by nuclear inspectors led to reports, not fines.

  • Cheaters pick the easiest way out.

    Lab tests report an average over the tricks they tried. A real cheater uses whatever works best, so the number that matters is the worst case. For GPU monitoring that’s about 43%, not 98%.

  • Checks don’t simply add up.

    A cheater looks for one path past all the checks, for example small hidden sites plus broken chip checks. Chip meters rely on chip checks, so if one fails, both do.

  • A photo flag is only the start.

    After a satellite flags a site, someone must inspect it and be let in. With 1% false alarms across thousands of factories, that’s hundreds of visits. Inspection capacity, not the 98.6%, sets the real catch rate.

  • Between countries, fines aren’t the point.

    For satellites, or chip checks on another country, the cheater wants to get ahead, not save money, and the punishment isn’t a fine. The real question is whether cheating is caught before the training run is finished.

  • Small under-reporting isn’t the safety risk.

    The “shaving” in step 2 matters for permit money. For safety, the question is whether a whole dangerous training run can be hidden.

  • Big runs are hard to hide in small sites.

    Satellites mostly miss sites under about 10 MW (around 7,000–10,000 GPUs). A frontier run needs around 100,000 GPUs, so it would need 10 or more hidden sites working together.

Word list

Compute
The computing power used to train an AI model, roughly how many calculations its chips did. Bigger training runs use more.
Permit
In this model, labs pay for each unit of compute they use. Under-reporting saves that payment. (This idea comes from Joel Christoph’s paper on compute permit markets.)
Check
Any way a regulator tests a lab’s report: an outside checker, a chip meter, a satellite photo. Also called an audit or an inspection.
Catch rate
If a lab is checked, the chance the check spots cheating.
Pass-or-fail check
A check that either finds the cheating or doesn’t, like a satellite spotting a hidden site.
Measuring check
A check that reads a number, like a meter counting compute, and compares it with what the lab reported.
Meter error
How far a meter’s reading can be from the truth, by accident.
False alarm
When an honest lab gets flagged. The regulator sets how often it’s willing to let this happen.
Shaving
Under-reporting by a small amount that stays inside normal meter error, so it’s rarely caught.
Going easy (collusion)
When a checker paid by the lab looks the other way.
Fine limit
A fixed maximum on any one fine. California’s is $1m per violation; New York’s is $1m, then $3m.
Stand-in
A number taken from a similar setting (tax audits, nuclear inspections) because nobody has measured the AI version yet.
