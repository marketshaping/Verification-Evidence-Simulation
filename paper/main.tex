\begin{abstract}
% 45 to 90 words. Write last. No citations.
Proposals to verify rules on frontier AI compute assume that their instruments detect violations. We test this assumption. For instruments drawn from published proposals, we code the evidence on their measured performance and pair each with an analogue from a regime that has run in practice, such as nuclear safeguards or emissions trading. Of the nine rows coded so far, one reports a measured detection rate, from a test rather than field use. \addtext{A compliance model shows that the inputs that decide whether cheating pays are the least measured.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% 90 to 175 words.
Compute governance proposals rely on a small set of verification instruments: satellite imagery of data centres, on-chip attestation, compute metering, third-party verifiers and others. The proposals state what each instrument should detect. Few report how often it does, at what false-alarm rate or under what evasion. Without these numbers, policy makers cannot tell whether an inspection regime would deter a developer from under-reporting compute.

Our research question is what the existing evidence shows about the detection performance of compute verification instruments. We code that evidence directly where it exists. Where it does not, we turn to analogues from inspection regimes with a track record. We then feed the coded values into a compliance model to find the audit and penalty levels at which truthful reporting pays.

\section{Related work}
% 65 to 130 words. Three to five closest works.
Shavit \cite{shavit2023} proposes compute monitoring through on-chip firmware and sets design targets for detecting large training runs. Aarne, Fist and Withers \cite{aarne2024} describe on-chip mechanisms such as remote attestation. Wasil et al.\ \cite{wasil2024} survey verification methods for international AI agreements. Baker \cite{baker2023} draws lessons for AI treaties from nuclear arms control verification. Christoph \cite{christoph2026} models compute permit markets under imperfect monitoring; our simulation extends that model. These works propose instruments or draw analogies. None of them collects measured detection performance across instruments.

\section{Methods}
% 110 to 200 words.
We keep a case register of instruments from published proposals. An instrument enters the register if it passes four tests: (1) It appears in a published compute governance proposal, (2) It is one instrument: one object checked by one kind of observation, able to raise an alarm on its own, (3) It does not duplicate an existing row under another name, (4) It verifies a claim; restrictions such as export controls are not included.

For each instrument, a coder fills a row with the object verified, the method, the access needed, the threat model and any measured performance. The coding rules ask for a source and page or section for each empirical entry, with design targets, laboratory results and field observations kept apart. The coder then pairs the instrument with one or more analogues from other regimes and records the published target, measured results, inspection effort and known evasion. Two labels go with every pair: evidence confidence (high, medium, low or absent) and transfer confidence (high, medium or low, with a one-line reason). Each instrument is assigned two independent coders, and we compare their rows to fix the field definitions. \addtext{A final field tags which model input the row informs (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}).}

Each row informs one or more parameters of a compliance model in which a developer's expected penalty equals the audit probability times the detection probability \addtext{times the chance of punishment} times the fine \cite{christoph2026,kaplow1994}\addtext{; Appendix~\ref{app:sim} describes it}.

\subsection{Simulation}
\begin{addedblock}
All results use placeholder ranges, so they show which numbers matter, not true values \cite{simrepo}.

\emph{Multiplying catch rates overstates a regime.} A cheater picks the one route past all the checks that pays best. With route choice, the fine needed is 2.0 times the gain with middle-range inputs and 4.4 times in a worst case; multiplying the same catch rates gives 1.1 and 1.4 (Figure~\ref{fig:regime}). Cheaters break the chip checks or train at a hidden site, so satellites carry the regime and the meter's high laboratory catch rate barely matters.

\emph{Small, slow runs are the hard ones.} A 10\textsuperscript{26} FLOP run takes a month on 100,000 GPUs, or 2.7 years on 3,000 GPUs in small sites that imagery mostly misses. The fine needed rises from about 2 to 7--10 times the gain (Figure~\ref{fig:size}). Slow runs need checks that do not rely on seeing a site.
\end{addedblock}

\section{Discussion}
% 110 to 175 words.
Proposals assume detection rates that few sources measure, and none measures in an operating regime. The analogues we expected to fill this gap do not report them either. Instead, the rows record attacks that worked: TEE.fail passed NVIDIA's attestation check by relaying a genuine H100 report \cite{chuang2025}, and a laboratory team reported defeating all 198 tamper-indicating seals it studied \cite{johnston2002}. \addtext{The model adds that detection is one of three inputs that decide deterrence, alongside the size of the fine and the chance of punishment, neither of which has evidence for any instrument (Table~\ref{tab:evidence}). If a large developer gains more than a flat cap on fines, as in California SB~53 \cite{sb53} and the New York RAISE Act \cite{raise}, no checking rate deters it.}

The study has three limitations so far. Each row has one coder. The simulation runs on placeholder ranges. Five instruments cover a small part of the proposals. In the second half we will complete the second coder rows, add new instruments that pass the four tests and run the model with coded ranges \addtext{(Table~\ref{tab:plan})}.

\section{Conclusion}
% 45 to 90 words.
Measured evidence on the detection performance of compute verification instruments is close to absent. Our coded dataset makes this gap visible instrument by instrument. Our compliance model shows which missing numbers matter most for whether an inspection regime deters cheating. So far, only satellite imagery has a measured detection rate.

\subsection{What it is for}
The evidence dataset says what is known about each verification instrument. The simulation asks what that knowledge is worth: \emph{given what we have coded, would a rule backed by these instruments stop a developer from hiding compute, and which missing number decides the answer?} It is a tool for directing the evidence search and for testing claims made about verification regimes. It is not a forecast. Most inputs are still placeholders, and every input is labelled measured, stand-in or guess so that results can say what they rest on.

Table~\ref{tab:glance} summarises the simulation.

\subsection{How it works}
\paragraph{One developer, one check.} A developer can save some amount by under-reporting compute. If it is checked (chance $\rho$), the check catches the under-report (chance $d$), and a caught developer is punished (chance $q$), it pays a fine $a$ per unit hidden. Hiding pays when the saving $p$ exceeds the expected fine, so honest reporting needs
\[
  \frac{a}{p} \;\ge\; \frac{1}{\rho\, d\, q}.
\]
The right side is the \emph{fine needed}, as a multiple of the gain. \emph{Worked example:} a verifier checks every year ($\rho = 1$), catches about a third of misreporting ($d = 0.35$, from tax audits), and every caught developer is punished ($q = 1$). The fine needed is $1/0.35 \approx 2.9$ times the gain. If the verifier goes easy, keeping a quarter of its catch rate, the fine needed rises to about 11 times.

\paragraph{Two kinds of check.} Satellites and verifiers are \emph{pass or fail}: they find the violation or they do not. Meters \emph{measure}: they read compute with some error, and the alarm is set so an honest developer is wrongly flagged only rarely. Small under-reports that stay inside the error band are never caught, so a meter leaves a floor of hidden compute roughly the size of its error, whatever the fine.

\paragraph{Several checks at once.} A developer does not face each check in turn. It picks one way of hiding, a \emph{route}, and meets only the checks that can see that route (Figure~\ref{fig:routes}). For each route $r$ with extra cost $c_r$ and chance of being caught $P_r$, the fine needed is $(1-c_r)/(q P_r)$, and the regime needs the largest of these. Multiplying all catch rates together, as if every check saw every cheater, always gives a smaller number. That gap is the error Figure~\ref{fig:regime} measures.

\paragraph{Run size.} A hidden run of $G$ GPUs needs about $1.2\,G$ kW. The developer can split it across $k$ sites. Imagery finds a smaller site less often (half of sites at 3--10\,MW), but each extra site costs 1--8\% of the gain. The developer picks the $k$ that is hardest to deter. This produces Figure~\ref{fig:size}.

\paragraph{Uncertainty and sensitivity.} Every input is drawn from a triangular distribution over its range, 2,000 times, with a fixed seed. Results report the median and the 10th--90th percentiles. To find which unknown matters most, all inputs are drawn together and each one's rank correlation with the outcome is measured. A worst-case scenario replaces selected ranges with the weakest route the evidence knows about, such as the 43\% catch rate of GPU monitoring against the hardest disguised runs.

\subsection{What would change the answers}
In order of how much they move the results: the size of fines relative to the gain from cheating; how often detection leads to a penalty; how easily each check can be bypassed (breaking chip checks, hiding a site, a lenient verifier); and detection by site size. The first two have no evidence for any instrument yet. Table~\ref{tab:plan} sets out how the second half will fill these gaps.
